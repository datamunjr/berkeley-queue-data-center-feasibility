# Glossary

[← Back to the summary](./README.md)

Plain definitions for the energy terms in this study.

**Battery (grid battery)**
A large battery, often the size of a row of shipping containers or more, that stores electricity and releases it later. It doesn't make power. It shifts power from when it's made to when it's needed.

**"4-hour" or "8-hour" battery**
How long a battery can supply its full power before running empty. A 150 MW, 4-hour battery can deliver 150 MW for 4 hours, or 600 megawatt-hours in total.

**Berkeley Lab / the Berkeley study**
Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, a U.S. Department of Energy research lab. Its yearly *Queued Up* report collects the country's grid waiting lines into one public dataset.

**Building next door (co-location)**
Putting a data center right next to a power plant and connecting the two directly with a private power line, instead of (or in addition to) going through the public grid.

**Data center**
A building full of computers (servers) that run websites, apps, and AI. They use a lot of electricity, around the clock.

**Grid**
The network of high-voltage power lines, substations, and local wires that carries electricity from power plants to homes and businesses.

**Grid connection agreement**
A contract between a power plant and the grid operator that sets the terms for connecting, including what upgrades are needed and who pays. A signed agreement means a project is far along in the waiting line. Industry shorthand is "IA" (interconnection agreement).

**Grid engineering study**
The studies a grid operator runs to see what a new plant would do to the grid. Projects go through several before getting a grid connection agreement.

**Grid operator**
The organization that runs the grid in a region and decides when new plants can connect. Examples: ERCOT (most of Texas), CAISO (most of California), PJM (Mid-Atlantic and Ohio Valley), MISO (Midwest), SPP (central U.S.).

**Interconnection queue (the waiting line)**
The line of new power plants waiting for permission to connect to the grid. Projects join by filing a request, then wait for studies and agreements.

**Megawatt (MW), gigawatt (GW), terawatt (TW)**
Units of power, or how much electricity something can make or use at one moment. 1 gigawatt = 1,000 megawatts. 1 terawatt = 1,000 gigawatts. Very roughly, one megawatt can power a few hundred U.S. homes. All U.S. power plants together add up to about 1.3 terawatts.

**Megawatt-hour (MWh), terawatt-hour (TWh)**
Units of energy, or how much electricity is used over time. One megawatt running for one hour is one megawatt-hour. Electricity is usually priced per megawatt-hour.

**Power plant / renewable power plant**
A facility that makes electricity. In this study, mostly solar farms and wind farms, sometimes paired with a battery.

**Project company**
A company set up just to own one power project, often with a generic name like "Example Solar LLC." It can be hard to tell from public records who owns it.

**SB 6 (Texas)**
A 2025 Texas law about large electricity users, like data centers, connecting to the grid. Among other things, it adds state review when a big new user wants to take power directly from a plant that's already connected to the Texas grid, and it lets the grid operator require large users to cut back during emergencies.

**Share of yearly need covered**
How much electricity a plant makes in a year compared with what a data center uses in a year. Over 100% means the plant makes more than the data center uses over the year, but not necessarily at every hour, for example at night.

**Test data center**
The made-up 150 MW data center used in this study to judge every plant the same way.

**Wind farm, solar farm**
Groups of wind turbines or solar panels that make electricity. Solar produces only during daylight. Wind varies with the weather and often blows at night.
