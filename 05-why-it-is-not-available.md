# 5. Why 2 terawatts is mostly not available

[← Back to the summary](./README.md) · [← Previous: What didn't work](./04-what-did-not-work.md)

## The question

Berkeley Lab shows more than 2 terawatts of mostly clean power waiting to connect to the grid. Data centers are desperate for power. **Why aren't more data centers just building next to those projects?**

## The answer

**The 2-terawatt figure counts projects in line, not power that's actually available to a data center next door.**

Out of about 9,200 active entries and 57 planned data centers, after clear rules and a close look at 105 possible pairs, **10** were worth pursuing and **2** stood out. Here's where the rest went.

### 1. Most projects in line never get built

Of the projects that joined the line from 2000 to 2020, only 13% were running by the end of 2025, and about 75% dropped out. A big share of today's 2 terawatts will drop out too. Many projects are early-stage placeholders.

### 2. A lot of it is the wrong kind or the wrong size

About a third of the active entries are stand-alone batteries, gas, or other types that don't fit "solar or wind next to a data center." Many of the rest are too small to power a data center, or so large that they're already part of a big company's plans.

### 3. The good ones get bought fast

The plants that look best for this, meaning far along, rural, a known company behind them, and a battery planned, are exactly what the largest tech companies are already buying:

- Google and Intersect Power built **Quantum** in Texas with a Google data center next door.
- Google bought **Intersect Power** itself in March 2026, along with its project pipeline.
- Amazon bought **Sunstone Solar** in Oregon.

Those deals prove the idea works. They also take the best projects off the table.

### 4. The spreadsheet lags reality

Berkeley's data runs through the end of 2025. Several plants it lists as "waiting" were already running or sold by 2026. A list of stuck projects is always partly out of date.

### 5. Data centers don't move, and they don't sleep

The biggest data centers are already being built in places chosen for other reasons, such as land, fiber, water, and tax deals. They won't relocate to a solar farm 35 miles away.

They also run all day and all night. A solar farm, even with a battery, usually covers a first building of 50 to 100 MW, not a campus of a gigawatt or more. Several giant campuses in this study sat next to solar farms that would cover only about 6% to 28% of their yearly needs.

### 6. The hard parts aren't on a map

Running a power line a few miles is cheap. What actually decides these deals:

- who owns the power and whether they'll sell it,
- whether the county will allow a data center,
- whether the lights stay on at night, and
- whether there's a company solid enough for lenders to fund.

None of that shows up in a list of projects or a distance score.

## What this means

- **The idea is real.** Google's Quantum project in Texas shows that building a data center next to new solar and batteries works.
- **The opportunity is narrower than the headline.** For a mid-size data center, it's a short list of specific plants, mostly in rural Texas, not a 2-terawatt pool.
- **Timing matters.** The best candidates are being picked up quickly. A project that's available today may be sold next quarter.
- **The realistic version is a first building, not a whole campus.** A solar or wind farm with batteries can power the first 50 to 100 MW cheaply. The grid, or other power, covers the rest.
- **Maps and scores are a starting point.** Tools like [WattConnector](https://wattconnector.munjr.com/) are useful for finding the right counties and plants. The real work starts after the score.
